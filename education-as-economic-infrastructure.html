Why the World Bank should treat education as economic infrastructure

By Kwabena Adu Darko-Asare September 25, 2026 7 min read

Roads, ports and power grids create possibilities. Human capital determines how fully economies can use them.

The argument

The World Bank should put education closer to the center of development strategy where weak human capital limits the returns from physical infrastructure.

The World Bank committed more than $80 billion through the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development and the International Development Association in fiscal year 2025. Of that, roughly $4.4 billion was classified as education spending.

That is about 5.5 percent.

Transportation and energy, by comparison, received roughly $23.7 billion combined, more than five times the amount committed to education.1

There are good reasons for this. Developing economies need roads, ports, electricity grids and transportation networks. Businesses cannot flourish without reliable power. Farmers need roads to reach markets. Manufacturers need ports to reach customers.

But the allocation raises a more fundamental question about development.

What if the most important infrastructure is the person using it?

The World Bank should put education closer to the center of its development strategy, particularly in countries where weak human capital limits the returns generated by physical infrastructure.

The argument is not to stop building highways.

It is to build the school first.

The 5.5% question

In fiscal 2025, IBRD committed $40.9 billion, with $1.28 billion classified under education. IDA, which provides concessional financing to poorer countries, committed another $39.9 billion, including $3.16 billion for education. Together, education represented approximately 5.5 percent of their commitments.1

This comparison needs an important qualification. World Bank sector classifications do not capture every project that contributes to learning or human capital. Nor does a single year's commitments describe the Bank's entire education portfolio.

Still, the figures reveal something about development priorities.

Physical infrastructure attracts enormous amounts of capital because its economic logic is immediate. Build a road and transportation costs can fall. Build a power plant and electricity supply increases. Build a port and trade capacity expands.

Education works differently.

Its infrastructure is human.

Its returns accumulate slowly, across workers, businesses, families and eventually generations.

Yet those returns can be substantial. World Bank analysis finds that each additional year of schooling raises earnings by close to 10 percent on average across countries. The Bank also estimates that roughly two-thirds of the income gap between high-income and low- and middle-income countries can be accounted for by differences in human capital.2

A road is only as productive as the economy around it

Imagine the World Bank finances identical highways in two regions.

The first has workers with strong literacy and numeracy skills. Farmers can interpret market prices. Entrepreneurs can maintain financial records. Businesses can train employees to operate new machinery.

The second has severe deficiencies in foundational learning.

Both now have better roads.

But we should not automatically expect identical economic returns.

Infrastructure creates possibilities. Human capital affects how effectively people can exploit them.

The same logic applies elsewhere.

Broadband becomes more economically valuable when people have the literacy and digital skills to use it productively.

Electricity becomes more valuable when businesses have technicians, engineers and skilled workers capable of supporting more sophisticated production.

A modern hospital requires doctors, nurses, technicians and administrators.

A highway can connect a farmer to a larger market. Education can improve that farmer's ability to evaluate prices, adopt technology, access financing and manage a growing enterprise.

This is the multiplier that deserves greater attention.
